An antibody does not attach to a whole protein the way a hand grips a ball. It contacts one
small patch on the protein's surface. Three terms describe the parts of that arrangement, and
they are not interchangeable.

The **antigen** is the whole molecule the antibody is raised against. The **epitope**, also
called the antigenic determinant, is the specific region of that molecule the antibody actually
touches. The **paratope** is the part of the antibody doing the touching. Antibodies recognize
antigens through interactions between paratope and epitope, and an antibody generally recognizes
only a small region on the surface of a large molecule.

The practical consequence is that a single protein carries many epitopes. Two antibodies sold
against the same target may be binding entirely different patches on it, which is one reason
they can behave differently in the same experiment even though the catalog describes them the
same way.

An epitope is best understood as operationally defined by the antibody that binds it rather than
as a pre-existing labeled feature of the protein, and its boundaries are not sharp. Some epitopes
are formed by residues that sit far apart in the sequence and are brought together only when the
protein folds, which is part of why the shape a protein is left in by an experimental method
matters so much.
