An antibody does not attach to a whole protein the way a hand grips a ball. It contacts one
small patch on the protein's surface. Three terms describe the parts of that arrangement, and
they are not interchangeable.

The antigen is the whole molecule the antibody binds. The epitope, also called the antigenic
determinant, is the specific region of that molecule the antibody actually touches. The paratope
is the part of the antibody doing the touching. Antibodies recognize antigens through
interactions between paratope and epitope, and an antibody generally recognizes only a small
region on the surface of a large molecule.

The practical consequence is that one protein offers many such patches. An epitope is counted by
the antibody that recognizes it, and on the operational account a protein is taken to contain as
many epitopes as there are different monoclonal antibodies that can be raised against it, so two
antibodies sold against the same target may be touching entirely different parts of it even
though the catalog describes them the same way.

Epitopes are described as relational entities: they are defined in relation to the paratope that
recognizes them rather than as pre-existing labeled features of the protein, and deciding which
residues are functionally part of one takes a criterion supplied by the method used to look. Some
epitopes are formed by residues that sit far apart in the sequence and are brought together only
when the protein folds, which is part of why the shape a protein is left in by an experimental
method matters so much.
