Two vials arrive from the same supplier. One label reads IgG1 and the other IgM, and neither says
anything about what the antibody inside will stick to. Those labels describe how the antibody is
built below its two gripping tips, in the trunk of the Y.

That build is the antibody's isotype, or class. It is set by the constant region of the heavy
chain, the stretch that varies little between antibodies whose tips grip completely different
things. Humans make five: IgM, IgD, IgG, IgA, and IgE, with IgG divided further into the
subclasses IgG1 through IgG4.

Class decides what happens after the antibody has bound. The constant region is the part other
immune components recognize, so it governs which cells an antibody can recruit to destroy and
dispose of what it caught, and where in the body it travels: IgG is the principal isotype of
blood and extracellular fluid, IgA of secretions, including mucus and breast milk, and IgM is
always the first made in a response.

None of that describes what the antibody binds. Recognition lives in the variable region at the
tips, and the same variable region can end up on different constant regions, so two IgG1
antibodies can bind entirely unrelated proteins.

Detection reagents, though, see the class rather than the target. An antibody can be raised
against the isotype of another antibody, and one raised against a single purified isotype can be
stripped of whatever cross-reacts with the other classes. Labeled with a dye, antibodies of that
kind are the standard secondary reagents for reporting where an unlabeled primary antibody bound,
which is why a secondary matched to one class may not report a primary of another.

Knowing the isotype tells you how to detect an antibody and what it can do once bound. Whether it
binds the intended thing is a different question entirely, and what a control antibody of the
matching class establishes is a question about the assay that control is run in.
